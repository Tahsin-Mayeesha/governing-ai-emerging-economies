THE RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Governance starts
with the conditions of use.

AI governance depends on more than a list of ethical principles. It also depends on the knowledge, resources, institutions, and everyday practices through which AI is developed and used.

AI4Bangladesh examines those conditions through interviews with 32 stakeholders. This article brings its key findings together with four related publications on Bangladesh and South Asia.

The emphasis is specific: what the research identifies as a challenge, what stakeholders and authors propose in response, and what still requires evaluation.

SCOPE OF THE EVIDENCE

The cases center on Bangladesh, with regional perspectives from South Asia. They offer considerations for emerging economies with comparable conditions—not a representative account of all emerging economies.

Policy descriptions reflect the publications’ research periods. They are not a live inventory of legislation.

How to read the evidence across publications

The empirical studies use purposive expert or stakeholder recruitment and qualitative analysis. They are not population surveys. Expert experience with marginalized groups is different from direct participation by the people those frameworks aim to support.

AI4Bangladesh reports 32 interviews. Building Bridges and the SIPG report describe 36. The Oxford chapter’s abstract says 32, while its methods say 36. This discrepancy is retained, and interview counts are not pooled or treated as independent samples.

In the flagship and accountability papers, the enumerated role categories sum to 31 and 35 respectively, one below their stated samples. The reported totals are shown without inventing a missing category. Source pages are available for inspection.

Know Your Users is a conceptual and literature-based contribution. Its discussion of a farmer study refers to prior work; its proposed formative user study is future work.

EXPLAINABILITY

The explanation depends
on who needs it.

Know Your Users asks a practical question: what information is meaningful to a particular person, using a particular system, in a particular setting?

The paper distinguishes technical interpretability from explanations that people can use. Language, literacy, age, domain knowledge, and access to technology affect what may be understandable.

Its recommendations include qualitative user research, local-language explanations, familiar analogies, visuals, voice, demonstrations, and ways to engage with limited connectivity.

Select an audience to explore the article’s summary of these considerations. These are design recommendations, not results from an evaluated interface.

GENERATIVE AI & REGULATION

New capabilities.
Additional governance questions.

The Oxford chapter extends the discussion to generative AI. It combines stakeholder interviews with a review of policy approaches and examines how misinformation, deepfakes, copyright, and data practices interact with regional conditions.

Stakeholders did not express a single view on regulation. The chapter brings together concerns about protections, innovation, compliance costs, and the possibility of political misuse.

Information integrity

Misinformation, impersonation, and fabricated media raise questions about verification, accessible public education, and responsibility for harm.

Rights and ownership

The chapter examines uncertainty around consent, data provenance, and the ownership of AI-generated content.

Proportionate governance

Proposals include context-aware rules, third-party audits, regulatory sandboxes, infrastructure investment, and periodic review.

THE ACCOUNTABILITY FRAMEWORK

Connecting principles
to institutional arrangements.

Building Bridges proposes four interdependent components. Explore what each is intended to do, then inspect the original model.

A proposed framework, awaiting validation.

The authors explicitly state that this framework has not been tested in real-world settings. Its development relied on expert interviews, rather than direct co-design with the marginalized populations it aims to support. It also assumes willingness to establish and resource governance bodies.

View the proposed implementation sequence

The paper outlines a five-year sequence. It is an authors’ proposal, not a government commitment or a completed implementation.

  1. Year 1Draft legislation and establish an AI authority.
  2. Year 2Pass legislation and establish an investigating body with legal authority.
  3. Year 3Scale literacy programs and implement audit requirements.
  4. Year 4Develop sector-specific guidance and regional capacity.
  5. Year 5Review and refine the framework using implementation experience.

RESEARCH CONCLUSIONS

The work of governance
is both technical and institutional.

Across the publications, AI ethics is connected to concrete conditions: what people know, which data practices are followed, whether infrastructure is available, and how responsibilities are assigned.

The proposed responses combine education, human-centered design, data protections, participatory policy development, and independent review. The studies emphasize adaptation to context rather than assuming that a framework will operate identically everywhere.

The research identifies concerns and develops proposals. Evaluating those proposals with affected communities and in operational settings remains an important next step.

What was examined

Stakeholder perspectives, research literature, policy documents, and locally situated governance challenges.

What was proposed

Ethical principles, design considerations, institutional components, and implementation guidance.

What remains to test

Feasibility, effectiveness, community fit, and the outcomes of specific interventions.
